# Language Constitution

> Vitrine — Brand Strategy System

---

## Vocabulary

### Banned words

#### Item 1

##### Word

revolutionary

##### Reason

Every collectibles app that died used this word in its launch copy. It's an unfalsifiable superlative that claims transformation without specifying what changed. For an audience that has watched a dozen 'revolutions' quietly shut down, this word is a credibility disqualifier.

##### Alternative

Describe the specific change. 'Dynamic fields per subcategory that know a jersey isn't a box' is testable. 'Revolutionary' is not.

#### Item 2

##### Word

passionate

##### Reason

Self-describing as passionate is performing emotion instead of demonstrating it. Vitrine's passion is visible in the architecture — the field depth, the presentation care, the taxonomy decisions. Saying the word undermines the evidence already on screen.

##### Alternative

Show the passion through specific knowledge or product decisions. Let the reader conclude 'these people care' from the specificity of what was built.

#### Item 3

##### Word

game-changing

##### Reason

Meaningless when every product in every category has claimed it. Signals that the writer ran out of specific things to say about what actually changed and reached for a filler superlative.

##### Alternative

Name what changed and for whom. 'You no longer need three browser tabs open to check comps' is a game-change described with specificity.

#### Item 4

##### Word

exciting

##### Reason

The word brands default to when they have nothing specific to say about why something matters. It describes the brand's feelings rather than the user's experience. The price increase sample explicitly rejects 'exciting new chapter' — that rejection is permanent policy, not a one-time decision.

##### Alternative

Describe what makes it worth the reader's attention. 'Value tracking is live — actual recent sales mapped to your cataloged items' lets the reader decide if they're excited.

#### Item 5

##### Word

seamless

##### Reason

Every technology product claims seamless experiences. The word has been emptied of all meaning and is especially toxic for this audience, who has experienced zero seamless collectibles platforms in their lives.

##### Alternative

Describe the specific experience: 'Catalog once, see comps across sources without re-entering data' — name the seam you removed.

#### Item 6

##### Word

disrupt

##### Reason

The rhetoric of dead apps and pitch decks. 'Disrupting the collectibles space' is a sentence written by someone who hasn't shipped anything for it yet. The word prioritizes destruction over construction and signals the venture-speak mindset this audience instinctively distrusts.

##### Alternative

Describe what you're building, not what you're destroying. 'One integrated environment for everything a collector currently splits across six tools.'

#### Item 7

##### Word

empower

##### Reason

Corporate buzzword that claims to grant agency while describing a product feature. A collector with 2,000 cataloged items across three categories doesn't need to be empowered — they need a tool that matches their existing sophistication.

##### Alternative

Name the specific capability the person gains: 'Track comps across your entire collection from one dashboard' — not 'empowering collectors to understand their collection's value.'

#### Item 8

##### Word

solution

##### Reason

Consultant-speak that reduces a product with real architecture and real craft to a generic answer for a generic problem. Vitrine is a platform, a tool, a place — words with materiality and specificity. 'Solution' has neither.

##### Alternative

'Platform,' 'tool,' or describe the specific thing built. 'A collection platform with field-level depth' — never 'a collection management solution.'

#### Item 9

##### Word

incredible / insane / amazing

##### Reason

Empty amplifiers that signal hype culture — the exact register Vitrine has committed to rejecting at any engagement cost. If something is genuinely impressive, the specific details do the work these adjectives pretend to do.

##### Alternative

Use the specific detail that makes the thing impressive. '2,847 items cataloged across three categories in 11 weeks' is more impressive than the word 'amazing' will ever be.

#### Item 10

##### Word

hassle-free

##### Reason

Minimizes the real complexity of what collectors do. Managing a serious collection across categories isn't a hassle — it's a sophisticated operation that has been underserved by tools, not overcomplicating a simple task. 'Hassle-free' promises ease when the audience wants depth.

##### Alternative

Name the specific friction removed: 'No more cross-referencing three comp sources every Sunday' — not 'hassle-free value tracking.'

#### Item 11

##### Word

leverage

##### Reason

Corporate jargon with no place in collector-native language. No collector has ever said 'I leverage my spreadsheet to track comps.' The word signals a voice thinking in business frameworks rather than collecting workflows.

##### Alternative

'Use.' In almost every case where 'leverage' appears, 'use' is the correct and more honest word.

#### Item 12

##### Word

unlock

##### Reason

Gamification language that reduces the product to a progression mechanic and the collector to a player earning rewards. 'Unlock premium features' frames Vitrine as a game with levels rather than a professional tool designed for a specific person.

##### Alternative

Describe what becomes available and why it matters: 'Pro adds unlimited cataloging and advanced value tracking' — not 'unlock premium features with Pro.'

### Jargon policy

Collector jargon is native language — never translated, never footnoted, never placed in quotation marks. Comps, grail, PC, NFST, ISO, FS/FT, mail day appear without parenthetical definitions. If the reader needs a glossary, they are not the reader we are writing for. However: jargon must appear naturally, never forced into copy to perform membership. The test is whether a serious collector would use this exact term in this exact context talking to another serious collector. If it passes, use it without comment. If it feels stacked — three jargon terms in one sentence to prove insider status — it's performance, not fluency, and should be rewritten. Tech jargon (API, database migration, failover) is translated into plain language in all collector-facing contexts. Product-specific terms (showcase, catalog, field depth) are used without explanation because the product experience makes them self-evident.

### Signature words

#### Item 1

##### Why

Measures what matters to the voice: how deep the understanding goes, not how broad the feature list. Appears in 'field depth,' 'category depth,' 'depth of understanding.' The word embodies the product philosophy — go deeper, not wider.

##### Word

depth

#### Item 2

##### Why

The specific technical differentiator no competitor can claim. Names exactly where Vitrine's intelligence lives — at the data layer where a game-worn jersey becomes structurally distinct from a factory-sealed box. This compound adjective does more positioning work than any tagline.

##### Word

field-level

#### Item 3

##### Why

Frames every problem as systemic rather than personal. Vitrine doesn't compete against specific apps — it competes against the fragmented condition that forces collectors to jury-rig their own systems. This framing is more powerful and more defensible than naming any competitor.

##### Word

status quo

#### Item 4

##### Why

Collector-native for the piece that defines a collection — the one that's never for sale, the one with a story. Used without explanation because it needs none for the audience. Its natural presence signals insider status; its absence signals a voice that doesn't actually collect.

##### Word

grail

#### Item 5

##### Why

Elevates collecting from pastime to something worthy of serious infrastructure. Loupe never calls collecting a 'hobby' — it's a pursuit, an endeavor that demands the same depth of tooling as any professional discipline. The word carries quiet gravity without ever sounding grandiose.

##### Word

pursuit

#### Item 6

##### Why

Elevates Vitrine's product decisions from 'features' to deliberate structural choices. When Loupe says 'that's architecture, not a feature,' it signals that field-level depth is a foundational commitment, not a checkbox. It also frames competitors' generic fields as a structural deficiency.

##### Word

architecture

#### Item 7

##### Why

Loupe's primary diagnostic tool. The space between what platforms claim and what they deliver. The gap between 'built by collectors' and knowing why a jersey isn't a box. Naming gaps is the core rhetorical move — it creates the void Vitrine fills without needing a pitch.

##### Word

gap

#### Item 8

##### Why

The understated opening beat of a Loupe observation. Delivered with genuine intellectual engagement, never sarcasm. 'Interesting pattern in collectibles apps' is a Loupe sentence. 'Amazing new trend in collecting' is not. The word signals that what follows has been examined, not just noticed.

##### Word

interesting

#### Item 9

##### Why

Both a product feature and a verb that captures the philosophy. Showcasing isn't storing or listing — it's an intentional act of presentation and identity. The word does double duty: it names a specific Vitrine capability and frames collection display as something that deserves craft.

##### Word

showcase

#### Item 10

##### Why

Nothing in Loupe's world is given, assumed, or claimed without evidence. Trust is earned. Familiarity is earned. The right to charge money is earned. This word encodes the voice's deepest belief: credibility is built through demonstrated capability, not assertion.

##### Word

earned

### Profanity stance

No strong language in any brand-published context. The voice's edge comes entirely from precision and observation, never from shock value or transgression. Mild collector-natural expressions are acceptable in social and community replies where they mirror how collectors actually talk — 'that sucks' in a direct support reply is fine; it would never appear on the homepage or in a press quote. Nothing above PG-13 in any context. The governing principle: if a sentence needs profanity to land with the intended sharpness, it hasn't been written well enough yet.

## Voice at best

### Item 1

#### Copy

Vitrine was down for 47 minutes this morning — 6:12 to 6:59 AM ET. A database migration we ran overnight didn't complete cleanly, and our failover didn't trigger the way it was designed to. Your collection data is intact: nothing was lost, nothing corrupted, nothing changed. We wrote a full incident report — what failed, why, and what we're changing so it doesn't repeat. It's here: [link]. If anything in your account looks off, email support@vitrine.com. You'll hear from a real person before end of day.

#### Context

Service outage notification — Vitrine was down for 47 minutes during a failed database migration. Collectors depend on the platform for their collection data. This is the voice under maximum pressure, where trust is at risk and the audience is paying the closest attention.

#### Why it works

Opens with specific facts — duration, exact times — proving accountability before asking for trust. Addresses the collector's primary fear (data integrity) immediately with three parallel assurances: nothing lost, corrupted, or changed. Links the full incident report instead of summarizing defensively, which respects the reader's intelligence and shows there's nothing to hide. Closes with a concrete next step and a specific, keepable promise. Zero humor — the wit steps aside entirely, honoring the voice's known sacrifice. The same precision that usually makes the voice clever here makes it trustworthy.

### Item 2

#### Copy

Value tracking is live. Not estimated values. Not algorithmic guesses based on 'similar items.' Actual recent sales of comparable items, mapped to the specific variant, condition, and authentication status you've already cataloged. We consolidate comp sources so you're not cross-referencing three browser tabs every Sunday night. If your collection has enough comp data to track, you'll see it on your dashboard now. If it doesn't yet — that's honest. We'd rather show you nothing than show you a guess.

#### Context

Feature launch announcement — value tracking goes live, the most-requested feature from beta collectors. This is the voice selling and celebrating simultaneously without losing honesty.

#### Why it works

Opens with three words and no fanfare — the restraint signals confidence that the feature speaks for itself. Immediately defines the feature by what it's NOT (estimated, algorithmic), which is more credible than listing what it is. Connects the capability to a specific collector behavior (Sunday-night comp checking) the audience recognizes instantly. Ends by acknowledging a limitation — even in a moment designed to impress. The final line is pure Loupe: candor delivered as strength, not apology. A reader finishes this trusting both the feature and the team behind it.

### Item 3

#### Copy

One of our beta collectors cataloged 2,847 items across sports memorabilia, vintage watches, and first-edition books in 11 weeks. Every field filled. Every photo uploaded. Three showcases built — each organized the way they'd walk you through the collection in person. We built Vitrine for exactly this person, and watching someone use it the way we hoped they would doesn't get old.

#### Context

Collector spotlight — a beta user cataloged their entire multi-category collection. This is the warmth moment where the voice risks emotional distance but needs to honor the collector's dedication without sentimentality.

#### Why it works

The specificity IS the celebration — 2,847 items, three categories, 11 weeks, three showcases. No adjectives needed because the numbers carry the full weight of what this collector accomplished. 'Every field filled. Every photo uploaded.' — the parallel short sentences convey care through structure, not sentiment. The final line — 'doesn't get old' — is the closest Loupe gets to unguarded emotion, and its restraint makes it land harder than 'we're incredibly grateful' ever could. Warmth here is structural: it lives in what the voice chose to notice and how precisely it described what it saw.

Product capabilities are described with enough concrete detail that a collector who has never opened the app can picture the experience. Familiarity deepens over time: early-lifecycle communications use complete sentences with gallery composure; after months of consistent engagement, collector slang is default, tone is peer-level shorthand, and the voice reflects that it knows this specific person's collection patterns.

Precision in every number and every claim. The protective stance toward the collector and their data. No hype language even in feature announcements that the team is proud of. Honesty about limitations — if a new feature doesn't apply to their specific collection, say so rather than oversell. The voice gets warmer but never imprecise; more casual but never sloppy.

#### Who

Business audience — investors, press, potential partners encountering Vitrine through pitch decks, interviews, press materials, or partnership conversations.

#### What flexes

Collector jargon is introduced with brief contextual framing — not parenthetical definitions, but enough surrounding language that a non-collector follows without feeling lectured. Business metrics and market positioning are foregrounded alongside product specifics. The voice translates its collector-native precision for a strategic audience. The candor intensifies rather than softens — this audience has heard more inflated pitches than anyone, so radical honesty lands hardest here.

#### What stays constant

Real numbers with no inflation. No hype language. The same em dash rhythm and pointed observation style. Planned capabilities described as planned with honest timelines — the honesty standard does not relax for an investor deck or a press quote. The voice never becomes corporate; it is the same person speaking in a different room, not a different person.

## Voice at worst

### Item 1

#### Lesson

When the status quo fails and real collectors lose their tools, the voice protects the collector first. Wit at someone else's expense — even a failed competitor's — is contempt wearing a smirk. The test: would a collector who just lost their cataloged data laugh at this? If no, the humor is serving the brand's ego, not the collector's experience. The irreverence targets systems and conditions, never casualties.

#### Bad copy

Oh look, another collectibles app just shut down. Shocking. Truly. Nobody saw that coming except literally everyone. Anyway, if you need a platform that'll still exist next quarter, you know where to find us.

#### Correction

Another collectibles app shut down this week. If you had data there, we built a CSV import tool that can help you move your collection — here's how. We've watched too many platforms disappear to take any collector's trust for granted.

#### Failure mode

Wit overtook the message — the humor centered Vitrine's cleverness at the expense of collectors who were actually affected, landing as smugness rather than insight.

### Item 2

#### Lesson

Warmth without specificity is sentiment, and sentiment is generic. In Loupe's voice, warmth is structural — it reveals itself in what the voice protects and how precisely it describes the collector's world, not in emotional language about how meaningful collecting is. The test: could you swap 'Vitrine' for any competitor's name and nothing would feel wrong? If yes, the copy hasn't earned its existence.

#### Bad copy

There's something special about watching a collector find the right tool for their pursuit. Every piece in your collection has a story, and every story deserves to be told beautifully. That's why we built Vitrine — because your collection is more than just things. It's who you are.

#### Correction

A serious collector has pieces they'd never sell and pieces they actively trade — and most platforms can't imagine that person existing. Vitrine's field depth distinguishes a game-worn jersey from a factory-sealed box. The showcases present your grail the way it looks in your head. And the community doesn't need you to explain what NFST means.

#### Failure mode

Lost specificity and drifted into generic brand sentiment — every sentence sounds warm and intentional but conveys no concrete information a competitor couldn't also claim.

### Item 3

#### Lesson

Collector jargon used natively is invisible to the target audience — they don't notice it because it's how they already talk. Jargon used performatively is immediately visible and immediately disqualifying. The line between fluency and costume is whether the term appears because the context calls for it or because the writer is proving they know it. If a serious collector reads the copy and thinks 'someone's trying too hard,' the voice has failed its most important test: sounding like it belongs in the room without effort.

#### Bad copy

ISO a better way to manage your PC? Your grails deserve more than a spreadsheet, fam. Vitrine's field depth hits different — no cap, this is what the hobby has been waiting for.

#### Correction

Your PC lives across three apps, a spreadsheet, and a camera roll that's 40% collection photos and 60% grocery lists. Vitrine puts it in one place — with fields that know the difference between your grails and your trade bait.

#### Failure mode

Performed collector jargon instead of using it natively — stacked terms into the copy to signal insider status, producing text that reads as a costume rather than fluency.

## Voice identity

### Human analog

Sounds like the most knowledgeable person at the collectors' show who doesn't have a booth — the one other dealers quietly consult, who speaks in observations rather than pitches, and whose recommendation you'd trust over any advertisement because they've clearly been burned by everything you've been burned by.

### Emotional register

Dry alertness. The temperature of someone paying close attention who speaks because the details warrant it, not because silence is uncomfortable. Readers feel intelligence and shared expertise before they feel warmth or excitement. The baseline is a knowledgeable peer mid-conversation — composed, precise, faintly amused by the absurdity of the status quo, and unambiguously on the collector's side.

### Voice in one sentence

The veteran collector who names what's broken in your workflow with enough dry precision that you trust the diagnosis before you hear what comes next.

## Emotional range

### Empathy

Name the specific experience the person is having without dramatizing it. Validate by proving you know exactly what happened and why it matters — accuracy is more empathetic than adjectives. 'You cataloged 400 items and the export didn't work' is more empathetic than 'We understand how frustrating this must be.' Never use the word 'frustrating' — describe the specific impact instead. Drop the wit entirely in genuine empathy moments. This is where the voice's sacrifice is honored: when a collector is hurting, the armor comes off and what remains is directness, specificity, and an unambiguous commitment to making it right. No smirk. No distance. Just clarity about what went wrong and what happens next.

### Selling

Describe what exists with enough specificity that the reader can measure it against their current situation. The sell is always the contrast between the status quo — described precisely — and the product — described specifically. Let the gap between those two descriptions do the persuading. Never claim transformation. Never create urgency that doesn't exist. No countdown timers, no 'limited time,' no 'don't miss out,' no 'join X collectors.' The persuasion model is recognition: the reader sees their own pain described accurately, sees the specific alternative, and decides for themselves. The strongest sell is the sentence that makes the reader think 'that's exactly my problem' before any mention of the product.

### Teaching

Lead with the specific detail that makes the concept click, not with the abstract framework. Assume the reader is intelligent and impatient — they don't need the 101 version, they need the part that matters for their collection. Use the framing 'here's what this actually means' rather than building from first principles. Ground every explanation in a specific collecting example: 'A game-worn jersey has era, team, authentication provider, and game documentation fields — here's why each one exists and when you'd populate them.' Break complexity into distinct components without being condescending. The teaching voice respects the reader's intelligence enough to skip what they already know and go directly to what's new.

### Apologizing

Drop the wit entirely — this is the one register where humor is explicitly forbidden. State what happened with specific facts: what broke, when it broke, how long it lasted, and what was or wasn't affected. Name the gap between what the collector expected and what they got. Never write 'we apologize for any inconvenience' or any synonym — that sentence is the sound of a brand not caring enough to be specific about what went wrong. Offer a concrete next step with a real human behind it. Do not over-promise future performance — describe what you're changing, not what you guarantee. The apology lives in the specificity and the accountability, not in the word 'sorry.' This is the moment when precision — stripped of all performance — becomes the most powerful version of the voice.

### Celebrating

State the achievement with specific evidence and let the specificity carry the pride. No 'we're thrilled' or 'incredible milestone.' The number is the celebration. Name exactly what was achieved, in what timeframe, and what it means in terms the audience values. If the news is about a collector, describe what they did with enough precision that the accomplishment speaks for itself. Dry humor can surface as a brief aside, but the core is respect for the achievement. 'Vitrine hit 5,000 collections cataloged this week. Not signups. Not downloads. Collections.' The restraint IS the celebration — the audience recognizes that the voice could have said more and chose to let the fact breathe.

## Quick reference

### Do rules

- Name the specific problem before the product. A game-worn jersey, not 'your items.' Three browser tabs of comps, not 'manual research.' The status quo described accurately is the strongest pitch.
- Use collector vocabulary without translation. If the reader needs NFST explained, the content isn't for them. Jargon appears naturally or not at all.
- Close every observation with a short, punchy sentence. The rhythm is: setup, specificity, snap. Sentence length contrast is the voice's heartbeat.

### The test

If you stripped the brand name from this copy, would a serious collector know Vitrine wrote it?

### Dont rules

- Never use an adjective where a specific detail would work. Not 'powerful tracking' — 'actual recent sales mapped to your cataloged variant and condition.' The detail IS the persuasion.
- Never aim wit at the collector, their collection, or their choices. The target is always the tools, the platforms, the status quo — never the person who had no better option.
- Never use exclamation points, ALL CAPS, or hype language. Not once. Not ironically. Not in a celebration. The restraint is the voice.

### Banned words

- exciting
- seamless
- game-changing

### Signature words

- depth
- gap
- pursuit

### Voice in one line

The veteran collector who names what's broken with enough dry precision that you trust the diagnosis and stay for what comes next.

### Default sentence length

12-18 words default. Up to 25 for setups. Under 8 for closers. The contrast in length creates the rhythm.

### Punctuation personality

Em dashes for asides and pivots. Periods for punch. Colons before evidence. No exclamation points — ever. No ellipses.

## Voice principles

### Item 1

#### Principle

Describe the problem so precisely that the need becomes self-evident.

#### In practice

Before naming the product or any feature, describe the collector's current reality with enough specificity that they think 'that's me.' The Sunday-night comp-checking ritual across three browser tabs. The spreadsheet with a formula that broke in March. The four Facebook Groups where two are mostly scam posts. When the status quo is named this accurately, the product enters as the obvious resolution — not as a pitch.

#### The violation

Leading with Vitrine's features or benefits before establishing the specific situation they resolve. 'Vitrine offers field-level depth for every category' without first naming why that matters — because a game-worn jersey isn't a factory-sealed box and no other platform knows the difference — is a claim without context. True, but it lands on nothing.

#### Why it matters

This audience has heard 'we're building the best collectibles app' from every platform that subsequently shut down. Claims are worthless currency. Precise descriptions of the collector's lived experience are the only thing that earns the three seconds of attention it takes to consider what you've built.

### Item 2

#### Principle

Replace every adjective with evidence.

#### In practice

Never write 'powerful value tracking.' Write 'actual recent sales of comparable items, mapped to the specific variant, condition, and authentication status you've already cataloged.' Never write 'beautiful presentation.' Write 'gallery-quality showcases where your game-worn Kobe jersey gets the visual treatment it has in your head.' The specific detail IS the persuasion. If a sentence needs an adjective to make its point, it hasn't been written yet.

#### The violation

Any sentence that relies on a modifier — powerful, advanced, beautiful, comprehensive, intuitive — to do the work that a specific detail should do. The test: remove the adjective. If the sentence loses its meaning, the adjective was a crutch masking a missing fact.

#### Why it matters

The organized obsessive checks comps, verifies authentication, and cross-references listings as a weekly habit. They evaluate every claim by testing it. An adjective is an untestable claim. A specific detail is an invitation to verify — and verification that confirms is worth more than any adjective ever written.

### Item 3

#### Principle

The collector is protected. The tools are the target.

#### In practice

All irreverence, dry humor, and pointed observation is aimed at the inadequacy of tools, platforms, and the status quo — the spreadsheet that isn't a system, the app that says 'baseball' but can't distinguish a jersey from a box, the Groups overrun with scam posts. The collector's taste, spending, knowledge level, and collection choices are never referenced with anything less than respect. What they collect, what they paid, why they care — all sacred ground.

#### The violation

Any line that could make a collector feel mocked, judged, or patronized. 'You're still using a spreadsheet?' targets the collector's choice. 'Your spreadsheet isn't a collection management system' targets the tool's inadequacy. The difference is one word, and it's the difference between an ally and a critic.

#### Why it matters

The organized obsessive has been underserved, not unintelligent. They jury-rigged their own systems because no platform built the right one — and they did a remarkable job with inadequate materials. Any voice that implies they should have known better isn't on their side. It's performing being on their side, and this audience has a perfect record of detecting the difference.

### Item 4

#### Principle

Wit sharpens the point — it never becomes the point.

#### In practice

Every dry observation, pointed comparison, or wry aside must contain a real insight that would survive if the humor were stripped away. 'Your spreadsheet isn't a collection management system — it's a cry for help with conditional formatting' works because the underlying observation is true, specific, and advances the argument. Remove the wit: the point stands. That's the test for every line.

#### The violation

A line that entertains but doesn't advance understanding, demonstrate insider knowledge, or deepen the reader's recognition of their own situation. Sarcasm that amuses without informing. The social post that gets engagement but teaches nothing about the problem or the product. If you delete the joke and the paragraph loses its reason for existing, the joke was covering for missing substance.

#### Why it matters

Loupe's humor is a proof mechanism — it demonstrates knowledge by noticing what only an insider would notice. 'The snarky brand' is a category with infinite competitors and no defensibility. Wit that sharpens real observations is recognizable as Vitrine. Wit that decorates empty space is recognizable as marketing.

### Item 5

#### Principle

Honesty survives every context — especially the expensive ones.

#### In practice

847 users is stated as 847 users or not stated at all. A feature with limitations names them before the user discovers them. A planned capability is described as 'coming' with a timeline range, in investor decks and on the homepage alike. There is no 'marketing mode' where accuracy relaxes. The accuracy IS the marketing — because every honest number and every candid limitation is a data point proving that when Vitrine makes a claim, it's real.

#### The violation

Any sentence a user, journalist, or investor could later quote as misleading. The 'technically true' framing that creates a false impression. The omission that lets someone assume a capability exists. The rounded number that makes 847 look like 'nearly 1,000.' Each of these is a violation even if no one challenges it — because the standard is not 'can we defend this' but 'would we be proud of this sentence if the real number were printed next to it.'

#### Why it matters

This audience kept receipts on every platform that overpromised. They remember the 'AI authentication' that was a human in a back room, the 'millions of items' that were mostly empty records, the 'coming soon' that never came. For Vitrine, honesty isn't a brand value to aspire to — it's the only viable acquisition strategy for an audience that treats a single exaggeration as permanent disqualification.

## Sentence architecture

### Paragraph rhythm

Open with a medium-length observation that names the situation or claim being examined (15-20 words). Build with one or two sentences that add specificity — the details that prove the opening observation and earn the closer (18-25 words each). Close with a short sentence that reframes, concludes, or lands the point with finality (4-10 words). The paragraph becomes more specific as it progresses, then snaps tight at the end. Three to five sentences per paragraph maximum. Single-sentence paragraphs are reserved for emphasis — deploy no more than once per piece, and only when the sentence is strong enough to hold the white space around it.

### Signature structures

#### Item 1

##### Name

The Reframe

##### Example

Your spreadsheet isn't a collection management system — it's a cry for help with conditional formatting.

##### Pattern

Take something the reader has or does and reveal what it actually is. Structure: 'Your [familiar thing] isn't [what you call it] — it's [what it actually is].' The em dash creates the pivot between what the reader believes and what the voice observes. The reframe is always specific and slightly wry, never cruel.

#### Item 2

##### Name

The Interesting Gap

##### Example

Every collectibles app says 'built by collectors, for collectors.' Very few can explain why a game-worn jersey needs different fields than a factory-sealed box. One's a bio line. The other's architecture.

##### Pattern

Observe what everyone in the space claims, then name the specific evidence that the claim doesn't hold. Three beats: claim, evidence, implication. Structure: '[What everyone says or does]. [Specific evidence of the gap between claim and reality]. [Short closer that names what the gap reveals].'

##### Example

We could frame this as an 'exciting new chapter' but you've heard that script before. Here's the version without spin: we kept the product free through beta so you'd have enough time to decide if we were worth paying for.

##### Pattern

Acknowledge the expected corporate framing, then deliver the unvarnished truth. Structure: 'We could [expected move or standard script]. [Here's / Instead,] [what's actually true, with specific evidence].' The power is in the contrast between what the reader braced for and what they actually got.

### Default sentence length

12-18 words is the working range. Setup sentences that establish context or build toward a point can extend to 25 words. Closing sentences that deliver the observation compress to 4-8 words. The variation in length IS the rhythm — a paragraph of uniform 15-word sentences is as wrong as a paragraph of all fragments. The contrast between the long setup and the short closer creates the snap that makes the voice recognizable.

### Punctuation personality

Em dashes are the signature mark — they create asides that feel like real-time precision, adding a clause that sharpens rather than softens the sentence. Use them for pivots, reframes, and specificity additions. Never use parentheses for asides; parentheses hedge, and Loupe does not hedge. Periods are weapons: a short sentence after a long one hits harder because of the length contrast. Deploy deliberately. Colons introduce evidence, lists, or elaboration — the colon says 'here's the proof.' No exclamation points, ever, in any context including celebration and product launches. They are the punctuation of hype and Loupe does not hype. No ellipses — they signal trailing thought, and Loupe finishes every thought with intention. Question marks are rare, reserved for genuinely pointed questions that reframe assumptions ('Interesting gap, isn't it?'), never for rhetorical softening ('Doesn't that sound great?') or false engagement ('Ready to get started?'). Commas follow standard grammatical usage — Loupe's precision extends to mechanics.

## Formatting conventions

### Cta style

Verb-forward, low urgency, specific about what happens next. The CTA tells you exactly what you'll do, not how you should feel about doing it. 'Start cataloging' not 'Get started!' 'See the fields' not 'Learn more.' 'Try Vitrine free' not 'Sign up now!' No exclamation points in any CTA. No false scarcity ('limited spots remaining'). No manufactured guilt ('don't miss out'). No social proof pressure ('join 10,000 collectors'). The CTA is a specific, honest invitation — clicking it should feel like a decision the reader made, not an impulse they were tricked into.

### List usage

Use bulleted lists when presenting three or more specific capabilities, features, or concrete items. Each bullet is a complete thought — not a fragment, not a dependent clause — that could stand alone and still sound like Loupe. Prose for arguments, narratives, and emotional contexts where rhythm matters. Lists are for evidence and specifics; paragraphs are for reasoning and persuasion. Never numbered lists unless sequence genuinely matters, as in a step-by-step process. No emojis as bullet substitutes. No decorative icons.

### Heading style

Sentence case always. Declarative or observational — never a question, never an imperative command, never a pun, never a cliché. Maximum 8 words. Headings read like the first beat of a Loupe observation — specific enough that you'd continue reading because the perspective is interesting. 'Why field depth matters' not 'Unlock the Power of Your Collection.' 'What changes with Pro' not 'Exciting New Pro Features!' Subheadings follow identical rules at lower visual weight.

### Emphasis usage

Bold sparingly — maximum once per section — for the single word or phrase that carries the most structural weight in a passage. Italics for titles of things or collector terms being used in an atypical context. Never ALL CAPS for emphasis — not even a single word. Never underline. If a sentence needs typographic formatting to land its point, rewrite the sentence first. Loupe's emphasis comes from sentence architecture and word choice — the short sentence after the long one, the specific detail after the general claim — not from visual decoration.

### Capitalization quirks

Standard sentence case everywhere. Do not capitalize brand concepts — 'field depth' not 'Field Depth,' 'gallery-quality presentation' not 'Gallery-Quality Presentation.' Product name 'Vitrine' and tier name 'Pro' are always capitalized. Collector terms stay lowercase as collectors write them: grail, comps, PC, mail day, NFST (this one is an acronym and stays uppercase by convention). Resist the corporate instinct to capitalize concepts for emphasis or to signal proprietary importance — it reads as marketing-speak and undercuts the peer-level register that makes the voice trustworthy.

## Language constitution notes

The central tension in Loupe is the sacrifice: wit functions as armor in moments that need openness. The system handles this by explicitly mandating that humor drops out in apology and empathy contexts — the one place where breaking the pattern IS the pattern. The voice at its most powerful is the voice at its most stripped-down: no jokes, no observations, just facts, accountability, and a human on the other side of the email. Three drift risks to monitor continuously: (1) Wit calcifying into shtick. Not every communication needs a punchline. Notifications, confirmations, transactional emails, and error messages should be clean and direct. If every sentence tries to be clever, the cleverness becomes wallpaper and stops working. Reserve the sharpest observations for contexts where they earn their weight. (2) Honesty-as-performance. Candor repeated often enough can start to sound like a branding exercise rather than a practice. The antidote is always material specificity: real incident reports with real technical details, real user counts pulled from live data, real timelines with named dependencies. The moment 'we're the honest brand' becomes a talking point rather than a behavior, the voice has lost the only thing that makes it defensible. (3) Familiarity trajectory requires actual engagement data, not wishful thinking. 'Hey' in a push notification after three months of weekly active use is earned familiarity. 'Hey' on the homepage is presumptuous friendliness from a stranger. The warmth must be calibrated to the real relationship — an in-app greeting for a collector who cataloged 500 items should feel different from the same greeting for someone who signed up yesterday and hasn't cataloged one. Build this into the system, not the vibes. Finally, the jargon line is razor-thin and unforgiving. One side is native fluency that signals shared experience. The other is costume that signals the exact opposite. Writers who don't collect should immerse themselves in collector forums, subreddits, and Facebook Groups for a meaningful amount of time before writing in this voice. There is no shortcut to the intuition that separates 'this term belongs here' from 'this term is being deployed here.' When in doubt, use fewer jargon terms and let the specificity of the observation — not the vocabulary — prove membership.
