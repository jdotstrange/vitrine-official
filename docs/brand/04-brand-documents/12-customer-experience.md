# Customer Experience

> Vitrine — Brand Strategy System

## Cx metrics

### Item 1

#### Metric

First-response specificity rate

#### Voice impact

When the first sentence names the exact problem — 'Your export dropped provenance fields' versus 'Thank you for reaching out' — resolution satisfaction increases because the collector immediately trusts the responder understood the issue. Specificity in the opening sentence is the single highest-leverage voice decision in support.

#### What it measures

Percentage of support responses that name the specific issue in the first sentence — versus opening with a greeting, a thank-you, or a generic acknowledgment. Proxy for whether the response sounds like a person who read the ticket or a template that processed it.

### Item 2

#### Metric

Resolution-to-advocacy conversion

#### Voice impact

The voice in a resolution — especially the honesty about what went wrong and the specificity of next steps — determines whether the collector walks away thinking 'they fixed it' or 'they actually understood my collection and what was at stake.' The second produces advocacy. The first produces neutrality.

#### What it measures

Rate at which collectors who had a support issue resolved become more active users afterward — measured by increased catalog activity, showcase publishing, or peer recommendations within 30 days of resolution. Tracks whether the support experience built trust or merely resolved friction.

### Item 3

#### Metric

Transactional message engagement depth

#### Voice impact

Transactional messages written in generic platform template voice get skimmed or ignored. The same messages written with Vitrine's specificity — naming the collector's actual category, referencing their catalog size, pointing to the specific new field — get read and acted on because they contain genuine information, not filler.

#### What it measures

Whether collectors take the suggested next action in transactional messages — clicking the export link, reviewing import results, checking new fields. Measures whether automated messages function as brand touchpoints or get deleted unread.

### Item 4

#### Metric

Win-back response rate

#### Voice impact

Win-back messages that lead with specific product changes ('three things changed since your last session') outperform messages that lead with the relationship ('we noticed you haven't been back'). The organized obsessive left because something didn't meet their standard. The voice that describes what changed — specifically enough to re-open the evaluation — earns another look. The voice that asks them to come back earns a delete.

#### What it measures

Percentage of lapsed collectors who re-engage after a win-back message — either logging back in, responding to the email, or exporting their data. Any action counts because all three indicate the message was read and evaluated.

## Experience notes

Three structural decisions worth naming for anyone building on this system. First: the wit-to-stakes inverse is the most important CX calibration in the entire brand. Marketing Vitrine runs at peak observational register — dry, pointed, slightly amused. CX Vitrine must be able to reach zero wit without feeling like a different brand. The connection is precision: in marketing, precision makes the voice clever. In crisis, the same precision makes the voice trustworthy. The through-line is the attention to specific detail, not the tone layered on top of it. Train support team members by having them read the outage notification sample and the value-tracking launch sample side by side — same voice, different rooms. Second: template gravity is the primary threat to CX voice quality. Every email platform, every support tool, every ticketing system comes with default templates that sound like every other company. The first draft of every transactional message should be written by someone immersed in the voice documents, then reviewed against the negative samples — specifically the 'template relapse' category. The test: if you could swap 'Vitrine' for any SaaS company name and nothing breaks, the message needs rewriting. Third: the data-export offer in cancellation and win-back messages is not a retention tactic. It is the CX expression of the brand's deepest commitment — that the collector's data belongs to them, and their presence in Vitrine is earned, not trapped. Every CX team member should understand that offering the exit is how Vitrine earns the stay. The moment anyone resists including the export path because 'it makes it too easy to leave,' the CX system has lost alignment with the brand constitution. One operational note: the at-risk intervention must be powered by real engagement data, not by time-based drip sequences. A collector who cataloged 500 items and stopped logging in after a bad field experience requires a categorically different message than a collector who cataloged 10 items and drifted away. The intervention message must reference their specific catalog, their specific categories, and — when possible — specific changes in the product that address the likely friction point. Generic re-engagement sequences are the CX equivalent of hype marketing: high volume, zero recognition, negative trust impact.

## Quality indicators

### Bad

- A support response where the brand name could be swapped for any competitor and nothing would feel wrong. 'Thank you for reaching out, we're looking into this and will get back to you soon' is every brand's response. It is no brand's voice.
- A transactional email that reads identically to the platform's default template — 'Your order has been confirmed' energy applied to 'Your account has been created.' If the message couldn't have been auto-generated by the email platform with zero customization, it hasn't been written yet.
- An apology that uses passive voice to obscure accountability: 'An issue was identified that may have affected some users' is corporate self-defense, not a brand speaking. Vitrine names what broke, when, who was affected, and what's being changed.
- A retention message that centers the brand's feelings — 'We miss having you in our community' — instead of delivering something the collector can use. If the message contains no specific product update, no comp data, and no field improvement, it's a guilt trip wearing a brand voice costume.

### Good

- A support response that could be read aloud by a real person without sounding robotic — the sentences flow as natural speech, not as assembled template blocks. The collector feels they're talking to someone who understood the problem, not someone who categorized the ticket.
- A transactional email that a collector forwards to another collector with the comment 'this is why I use this app' — the message contains something specific enough about their collection or their workflow that it functions as peer proof, not just a receipt.
- A difficult-conversation response where the collector replies with 'thank you, that's exactly what I needed to know' — indicating that the specificity and honesty resolved their concern without requiring a follow-up cycle of vague answers and escalation.
- A win-back or retention message that the collector saves rather than deletes — because it contained a genuine comp insight, a field update in their category, or a specific product change worth re-evaluating.

## Cx voice principles

### Principles

#### Item 1

##### Name

Precision is the empathy

##### Meaning

In CX, accuracy about what happened replaces emotional language about how someone feels. Naming the specific failure, the specific impact, and the specific next step communicates more care than any 'we understand your frustration' ever could. The collector who cataloged 400 items and hit an export bug doesn't need you to label their emotion. They need you to prove you know exactly what broke.

##### In practice

Every support response opens with the specific situation — not a greeting, not a thank-you, not an emotional acknowledgment. 'Your catalog export generated the file but the download link failed' is the first sentence. 'We understand how frustrating this must be' is never the first sentence, or any sentence. Describe the impact in terms the collector values: 'Your data is intact' matters more than 'we're working on it.' The specificity IS the care.

#### Item 2

##### Name

Stakes set the register

##### Meaning

The voice's wit, observational distance, and personality are inversely proportional to what the collector has at risk. General inquiry about how showcases work — full Loupe register, dry and specific. Question about whether their data survived an outage — zero humor, zero asides, only facts and next steps. The voice doesn't have a 'support mode' that's uniformly flat. It has a stakes-awareness that modulates naturally.

##### In practice

Before writing any CX response, identify the stakes: Is collection data at risk? Is money involved? Is something broken that blocks their workflow? High stakes means declarative sentences, no em dash asides, no observations about the status quo — just what happened, what it means for their collection, and what happens next. Low stakes means the full voice can surface: a question about cross-category cataloging gets the same knowledgeable-peer register as a blog post about field depth.

#### Item 3

##### Name

Close every interaction with a specific next step

##### Meaning

No CX communication ends without the collector knowing exactly what happens next, who does it, and when. 'We'll look into it' is not a next step. 'We're investigating the sync failure and will email you an update by 5 PM ET today — if you don't hear from us, reply here and a person will see it' is a next step. Specificity in the close is the final trust deposit.

##### In practice

Every response ends with: what will happen, by when, and how to reach a human if it doesn't. Timelines are honest ranges, never optimistic guesses. If the timeline is unknown, say so and name when you'll update them: 'We don't have a resolution timeline yet. We'll update you by noon tomorrow with what we know.' A specific, kept promise builds more loyalty than a fast, vague one.

#### Item 4

##### Name

The collector's system was intelligent — honor it

##### Meaning

Many CX interactions involve collectors migrating from spreadsheets, asking about importing data, or comparing their old workflow to Vitrine's. The voice never implies their previous system was wrong. It was a sophisticated response to inadequate tools. CX respects the ingenuity and helps translate it — not replace it with condescension.

##### In practice

When a collector asks about importing a spreadsheet with 30 custom columns, the response acknowledges the depth of what they built: 'That's a serious catalog — let's map your columns to Vitrine's fields and figure out which translate directly and which we'll need to handle differently.' Never: 'You won't need all those columns anymore.' They might. And dismissing them dismisses the years of work behind them.

### Cx vs marketing

#### Item 1

##### Cx

Names the specific situation — what happened, what's affected, what they asked — to prove the response is real, not templated. No scene-setting. No preamble. The situation IS the opening.

##### Dimension

Opening move

##### Marketing

Names the status quo — the fragmented workflow, the Sunday-night comp ritual — to create recognition before the product enters.

#### Item 2

##### Cx

Scaled to stakes. General questions and low-stakes interactions get the knowledgeable-peer register. Anything involving broken features, data risk, or financial impact gets zero wit — only precision, accountability, and a human on the other end.

##### Dimension

Wit and observational voice

##### Marketing

Full deployment. Dry observations, reframes, pointed comparisons with the status quo. The personality earns attention and proves insider knowledge.

#### Item 3

##### Cx

Shorter overall. Declarative and direct in high-stakes responses. Em dashes appear in low-stakes, conversational responses but disappear when something is broken. Paragraphs stay under three sentences. The rhythm serves clarity, not style.

##### Dimension

Sentence architecture

##### Marketing

Setup-specificity-snap rhythm. Medium observation, specificity build, short punchy closer. Em dashes for pivots and asides.

#### Item 4

##### Cx

Action-oriented with named owners and timelines. 'Reply here and a person will see it within the hour.' 'Email support@vitrine.com — you'll hear back before end of day.' The promise must be keepable.

##### Dimension

CTA and close

##### Marketing

Invitational, specific about what happens next, zero urgency. 'Start cataloging free.' 'See the fields in your category.'

### What we never do

- Never open with 'Thank you for reaching out' or 'Thanks for contacting us.' These are the sound of a ticket queue, not a person. Open with the situation or the answer.
- Never write 'We apologize for any inconvenience' or any variation. This is corporate liability language. If something broke, name what broke. If it affected their data, say so. If it didn't, confirm that first. The apology lives in the specificity and the fix, not in a stock phrase.
- Never use 'we understand your frustration' or 'we know how important your collection is to you.' Claiming to understand is not understanding. Describing the specific impact — 'your export failed after you'd spent an hour organizing it' — proves understanding without asserting it.
- Never deploy humor in a response where the collector's data, money, or workflow is at risk. Not reduced humor. Not gentle humor. None. The wit is armor, and this is the moment the armor comes off.
- Never blame the collector's setup, browser, or device before confirming the issue is on their end. 'Please try clearing your cache' as a first response is the CX version of 'you're holding it wrong.' Investigate first, then troubleshoot with specifics.
- Never end a response with 'Let us know if there's anything else we can help with.' It's filler. End with the specific next step, the specific timeline, and the specific way to reach a human.

### Cx voice description

In CX contexts, Vitrine sounds like the same knowledgeable collector — but the room has changed. Marketing is the show floor: observational, pointed, slightly wry. CX is the back table after the show: direct, specific, armor off when it needs to be. The dry precision stays. The collector-native fluency stays. What shifts is the ratio of observation to action. In marketing, the voice names the gap and lets the reader close it. In CX, the voice names what happened and closes it for them. The wit scales inversely with the collector's stakes — a general question about field depth gets the full Loupe register; a failed catalog export gets zero personality and maximum clarity. A collector reading a support reply should recognize the same intelligence they saw on the homepage, deployed differently because the situation demands it.

## Retention messaging

### Win back

#### Item 1

##### Sample

Three things changed in Vitrine since your last session. First: comp sources expanded for Vintage Basketball — your tracked items now pull from two additional auction databases. Second: game-worn subcategory fields added provenance documentation and photo-matching status. Third: showcase sharing now generates a standalone link you can drop into any Facebook Group or Discord. Your catalog is where you left it — 60 items, fields intact. If none of this changes your calculus, your data exports as CSV anytime. It's yours.

##### Timing

60 days after last login — long enough that the absence is deliberate, soon enough that their catalog data is still a sunk cost worth recovering

##### Approach

Lead with something that changed since they left — a field improvement in their category, a new comp source, a feature they requested. Make the message about the product earning another look, not about the brand wanting them back. Include a one-click path to their existing catalog. End with the export option, because offering the exit is the ultimate trust signal.

### At risk message

Your Vintage Basketball catalog has 60 items with comp tracking active — three of those items had significant movement this month. Your Jordan rookie comps updated twice since your last login. That data is on your dashboard now. We also shipped deeper subcategory fields for game-worn items last week — provenance documentation and photo-matching status are live. If the field depth in your category wasn't meeting your standard before, it's worth another look. If something else drove you away, we'd rather hear it straight: support@vitrine.com goes to a person.

### At risk signals

- Login frequency drops from weekly to monthly, then stops — particularly after a feature launch or pricing change they didn't engage with. The organized obsessive who used to check comps every Sunday and hasn't logged in for three weeks is making a quiet evaluation.
- Cataloging stalls significantly below their collection's likely size. A collector who described a 500-item collection and cataloged 60 before stopping hit a friction point — bad fields in their category, import difficulties, or a depth expectation that wasn't met.
- Showcase is built but never published or shared. The collector invested in presentation but decided the result wasn't worth showing to their community — a signal that the product didn't deliver the visual standard they expected.
- Support ticket about a core workflow issue (import failure, wrong fields, missing subcategory) with no follow-up engagement. They asked, the answer didn't satisfy, and they left without arguing — the quietest form of abandonment.

### Ongoing engagement

#### Item 1

##### Type

Comp trend summary — monthly email to active collectors with value tracking enabled

##### Sample

Your 90-day comp summary: 14 items in Vintage Basketball tracked, average movement +6%. Three items saw the most significant shifts — your Jordan rookie held steady, two role-player cards moved on recent auction volume. PSA 9s in your cataloged era outperformed raw cards by 8-12%. Full breakdown on your dashboard. If you're considering grading submissions, this is the context the market is giving you right now.

##### Purpose

Deliver the data they would have manually compiled, saving them the Sunday-night ritual and proving Vitrine's value tracking is worth the subscription. The email is useful even if they don't open the app — the data IS the engagement.

#### Item 2

##### Type

Collection milestone — triggered when a collector hits a cataloging depth threshold

##### Sample

500 items cataloged. Every field filled across three categories. Your catalog now has enough depth for comp trends to surface patterns a snapshot couldn't show. Your showcases updated automatically. Worth a look at how the collection reads now — it's different at this scale.

##### Purpose

Recognize the investment of time and care without celebrating in a way that feels performative. The number IS the recognition. Let it land with the restraint the voice demands.

### Win back principles

- Never guilt. 'We miss you' is the retention equivalent of 'we're excited to announce' — it centers the brand's feelings instead of delivering value. The collector doesn't owe Vitrine their attention. Vitrine owes the collector a reason to come back.
- Lead with what changed, not with what they're missing. The collector left because the product didn't meet a standard. The win-back must demonstrate that the standard has been raised, with specific evidence they can verify.
- Always offer the data export. A collector who knows they can leave easily is a collector who stays by choice. The export offer communicates confidence, not desperation — and it's the move no competitor makes because they're afraid of the answer.
- One message. If the win-back doesn't work, respect the decision. A second follow-up after a non-response crosses from value-delivery into harassment. They know where to find Vitrine if the next change is the one that earns them back.

### Intervention approach

Intervention is data-driven and specific, never generic. If a collector stalled at 60 items, the intervention references their specific category and asks whether the depth met their standard — not 'we noticed you haven't been back.' If login frequency dropped after a pricing change, the intervention acknowledges the timing without dancing around it. Every at-risk message must contain something genuinely useful — a comp update on items they already cataloged, a field improvement in their category, a showcase example from a collector with a similar collection. The usefulness is the intervention. If there's nothing useful to say, silence is better than a 'we miss you' email that communicates desperation instead of value.

## Difficult conversations

### Item 1

#### Samples

##### Item 1

###### Context

A bug in a value tracking update showed incorrect comp data for 6 hours before being caught

###### Response

Between 2 AM and 8 AM ET this morning, comp data for Vintage Basketball displayed incorrect values — some items showed comps from the wrong variant. A mapping error in our latest comp source integration matched PSA grades to BGS grades for a subset of items. Your cataloged data wasn't affected. Your comp history prior to this morning is accurate. The incorrect data has been removed and correct comps are live now. We've added variant-level validation to every comp source integration going forward — this specific error can't recur in the same way. If any comp data in your account still looks off, email support@vitrine.com. A person will check it against source data and confirm within 24 hours.

#### Never do

- Never write 'we apologize for any inconvenience' — it's a legal hedge, not an apology. Name the specific inconvenience.
- Never minimize scope to manage perception. If 2,000 collectors were affected, say 2,000. If you don't know yet, say 'we're still determining the scope and will update you by [time].'
- Never deploy wit, dry humor, or observational asides in any apology context. The voice's armor comes off completely here. What remains is directness and accountability.
- Never promise 'this will never happen again.' Promise specific changes that reduce the probability and name them.

#### Scenario

Apologizing — Vitrine caused a problem that affected collectors' experience or data

#### Structure

- Open with what happened — specific facts, specific times, specific scope of impact
- Confirm data integrity immediately
- Explain cause with enough technical clarity to be credible, translated into plain language
- Name what you're changing to prevent recurrence — not a promise it won't happen again, but the specific action being taken
- Provide a concrete next step and a real human to contact

#### Principles

- The apology lives in the specificity. Naming exactly what broke, when, for how long, and what was affected IS the apology. The word 'sorry' is hollow without the facts that give it weight.
- Address the collector's primary fear first. In almost every Vitrine failure, that fear is data integrity. Confirm their collection is safe before explaining what happened.
- Accountability means naming your failure, not hiding behind passive voice. 'A migration we ran' not 'an issue occurred.' 'We didn't catch it in testing' not 'it slipped through.'

### Item 2

#### Samples

##### Item 1

###### Context

A dealer asks for bulk upload tools, POS integration, and consignment tracking

###### Response

You're describing a dealer workflow — bulk operations, POS, consignment management. Vitrine isn't built for that. Our field-level architecture is designed for individual item documentation at collector depth, and building bulk operations would require flattening the very specificity that makes the product work for the collector it serves. For retail and consignment operations at your scale, Shopify plus a dedicated consignment system will handle the volume and integrations you need. If you also collect personally — separate from the business inventory — Vitrine's depth is built for that side of what you do.

#### Never do

- Never say 'not at this time' when the answer is 'not ever.' Ambiguity in a no creates false expectation that erodes trust more than a clean decline.
- Never frame the no as the collector's problem: 'that's not really what Vitrine is for' puts the misalignment on them. 'Vitrine's architecture is built for individual collector depth, not retail volume' puts it on the product's deliberate choices.

#### Scenario

Saying no — declining a request that doesn't fit the product's architecture or audience

#### Structure

- Name their request back to show you understood it fully
- State clearly that this isn't something being built, and name the specific reason
- If an alternative exists — in Vitrine or outside it — name it honestly
- Close without apology. A product with clear boundaries is more trustworthy than one that says yes to everything.

#### Principles

- A clear no with a specific reason respects the collector's time more than a vague maybe. 'That's not something we're building' is more honest than 'we'll consider it' when you won't.
- When saying no, explain the architectural reason — not 'we can't' but 'building that would require changing something that makes the product work for the collector it was designed for.' The no should reveal the product's values, not just its limitations.

### Item 3

#### Samples

##### Item 1

###### Context

A collector's catalog export lost custom field data — they spent weeks documenting provenance and the export came back with empty columns

###### Response

Your export dropped the provenance documentation fields — the CSV has your item names, conditions, and values, but the custom fields you populated are showing blank. That's not a formatting issue on your end. Our export function didn't map custom fields correctly in the latest update. Your data is intact inside Vitrine — nothing was deleted or changed. The export is what broke, not the catalog. We're generating a corrected export now that includes every field you documented. You'll have it at the email on your account within two hours. If it's still wrong, reply directly to this email — I'm handling this, not a queue.

#### Never do

- Never tell an angry collector to calm down, be patient, or remember that the team is 'working hard.' Their frustration is proportional to their investment. Respect it by fixing the problem.
- Never deflect with 'this is a known issue we're working on' without naming when it will be resolved for them specifically. 'Known issue' without a timeline is the sound of a company that cataloged the problem and shelved it.
- Never respond to anger with corporate warmth — 'we truly value you as a member of our community.' They don't want to be valued. They want their provenance data back.

#### Scenario

Angry collector — emotions are high, the message is heated, the frustration is real

#### Structure

- Skip any preamble. Open with the specific problem they described — prove you read the whole message, not just the anger
- Confirm what you know and what you're still investigating. Separate facts from unknowns clearly.
- Name the resolution path with the fastest realistic timeline
- Offer direct human contact — not 'reach out anytime' but a specific channel with a specific response commitment

#### Principles

- Anger is almost always a secondary emotion. Underneath it: fear that their data is lost, frustration that something they invested time in failed them, the pattern-recognition of another platform letting them down. Address the underneath, not the surface.
- Match their urgency with speed and specificity, not with emotional mirroring. They don't need you to feel what they feel. They need you to fix what broke, fast, and prove you understand exactly what happened.

### Item 4

#### Samples

##### Item 1

###### Context

The marketplace, originally estimated for Q3, is delayed to Q1 of next year

###### Response

The marketplace we scoped for Q3 is now targeting Q1 next year. The authentication integration — connecting buyers and sellers to verified authenticators at the transaction layer — is taking longer than we estimated. We could ship a marketplace without it, but a transaction layer without embedded authentication in collectibles is how trust breaks. We'd rather be late and right. Your catalog, showcases, value tracking, and community features aren't affected — those continue developing on the original timeline. Comp source expansion for watches and books ships next month as planned. The marketplace delay is a marketplace delay. It doesn't change what's live or what's next for V1. If this changes your evaluation of Vitrine, we understand. Your data exports cleanly anytime.

#### Never do

- Never bury bad news inside good news. 'Great things are happening at Vitrine, and also the marketplace is delayed' is manipulative framing the collector will see through instantly.
- Never blame external factors without naming your own role. 'Our partner delayed us' without 'and we underestimated the integration complexity' is deflection.
- Never describe a delay as 'taking more time to get it right' without naming specifically what's being gotten right and what the original plan missed.

#### Scenario

Delivering bad news — a feature is being removed, a category's depth isn't what was expected, or a timeline has slipped significantly

#### Structure

- State the bad news in the first sentence — no warm-up, no context-setting that delays the impact
- Explain the reason with enough specificity that the collector can evaluate whether it's legitimate
- Name the impact on their experience and what — if anything — they need to do
- State what's still on track and what the revised plan looks like
- Close with how to reach a person if they have questions the email didn't answer

#### Principles

- Bad news delivered with specificity is trusted. Bad news delivered with spin is remembered and held against the brand permanently. The collector would rather hear 'this is six months behind our estimate and here's why' than silence followed by a quiet deadline shift.
- Name what changed, why, and what it means for their specific workflow. Then name what hasn't changed — the commitments that still hold.

## Journey touchpoint language

### Pre purchase

#### Item 1

##### Moment

First website visit — collector landed from a peer recommendation or a social post that stopped their scroll

##### Sample

Your collection lives across a spreadsheet, three apps, and a camera roll you're afraid to scroll through in public. None of them know that a game-worn jersey needs different documentation than a factory-sealed box. Vitrine is one place — with field depth per category and presentation your pieces actually deserve. Catalog one item. The fields will tell you whether we got it right.

##### Voice priority

Knowing. The voice must prove insider fluency within the first three seconds — collector-native vocabulary, a specific observation that only someone who manages a cross-category collection would make. The recognition moment does the acquisition work.

##### What they need

Recognition of their exact situation before any product mention. Evidence they can verify in five minutes — field-depth screenshots in their category, real numbers, honest limitations. A reason to catalog one item, not a reason to commit.

##### Emotional state

Guarded optimism layered on scar tissue. They've been here before — the app that 'got it,' that lasted six months. They want this to be different but have trained themselves not to believe anything until they test it. They're reading every word for the tell: hype language, vague claims, inflated numbers. One signal that this is another pitch and they're gone.

#### Item 2

##### Moment

Comparing Vitrine to their current system — mentally mapping their spreadsheet columns to Vitrine's fields

##### Sample

Sports memorabilia is our deepest category — a game-worn jersey surfaces era, team, authentication provider, and game documentation fields automatically. Vintage watches and first-edition books are live with specialist-validated fields. Three subcategories are still in development — check the depth status page for exactly what's ready. CSV import maps your existing columns to our fields. Start with 10 items in your primary category and see if the depth holds.

##### Voice priority

Precisely confident. Show the depth through specific subcategory examples, not through superlatives. Acknowledge what's still being built without apologizing for it.

##### What they need
