# Messaging Frameworks

> Vitrine — Brand Strategy System

## Tagline system

### Donts

- Never append 'for everyone' or broaden language to include casual collectors — the specificity is the membership signal
- Never lead with technology descriptors ('AI-powered,' 'cloud-based') — the audience cares about collecting intelligence, not infrastructure
- Never use exclamation points, ALL CAPS, or hype punctuation in any tagline context
- Never position taglines around marketplace or transaction functionality until V2 is live and proven
- Never replace collector-native terms with marketing synonyms — 'comps' not 'market data,' 'grail' not 'prized item,' 'depth' not 'comprehensive features'

### Primary

#### Use

Hero placement on website, app store primary tagline, social media bios, top of investor decks, and any context where Vitrine introduces itself for the first time. This is the front door.

#### Tagline

Your whole collection. Finally home.

#### Communicates

'Whole' signals cross-category completeness and the complete-collector thesis. 'Finally' validates the long search and abandoned apps — a word only the organized obsessive feels the weight of. 'Home' establishes permanence, identity, and belonging that no competitor credibly claims. The phrase is a membership test: if 'finally' resonates, you're who we built for.

### Explanatory

#### Use

Below the primary tagline on the website, in app store descriptions, in marketing materials where the audience needs specificity about what the product actually does. Pairs with the primary to move from emotional resonance to concrete, testable promise.

#### Tagline

Field-level depth. Gallery-quality craft. Everything you collect, in one place.

#### Communicates

The three structural differentiators in order of distinctiveness: subcategory-level field intelligence, gallery-quality presentation, and cross-category unity. Each phrase is testable within five minutes. No softening words, no marketing jargon — collector-native language throughout.

## Messaging donts

### Never do

- Never describe a planned feature in present tense — the marketplace, the Verification Hub, or any capability not yet shipped. 'Coming in Q3' is honest. 'Buy and sell through a marketplace' when the marketplace doesn't exist is a violation that will be discovered and remembered permanently. This applies to website copy, app store descriptions, investor decks, social posts, and press quotes without exception.
- Never use an exclamation point in any brand-published context — not in headlines, not in CTAs, not in celebration posts, not in push notifications, not ironically. The restraint is the voice. An exclamation point is the punctuation of hype, and Vitrine does not hype.
- Never compare Vitrine to a specific competitor by name in any public-facing content. Name the status quo, the fragmentation, the structural gap — never the company. 'Every platform forces you to choose' is positioning. 'Unlike [Competitor], we offer...' is a feature comparison that makes both brands smaller.
- Never round up a real number to sound more impressive. If you have 2,300 users, say 2,300 — not 'thousands,' not 'nearly 2,500,' not 'over 2,000.' The real number IS the marketing. The restraint in reporting it honestly IS the trust signal.
- Never use collector jargon performatively — stacking terms like 'ISO a better way to manage your PC? Your grails deserve more' to signal insider status. Jargon appears naturally or not at all. The test: would a serious collector use this exact term in this exact context talking to another serious collector? If the sentence sounds like it's trying to prove membership, it's performance, not fluency.

### Never say

#### Item 1

##### Why

The corporate preamble that every brand uses before every announcement. It centers the brand's feelings instead of the thing being announced. It's ten words of nothing before the sentence starts. The organized obsessive reads past it — and the fact that you opened with it tells them this is marketing, not information.

##### Instead

Open with the thing itself. 'Value tracking is live.' 'Vintage watches are in.' 'Pro starts next month.' The announcement IS the opening. Let the specifics earn the reader's response.

##### Pattern

'We're thrilled / excited / proud to announce...'

#### Item 2

##### Why

Broadening language that signals the brand is trying to be everything to everyone. The organized obsessive hears 'we don't know who you are specifically, so we're casting wide.' It's the opposite of the recognition moment — it's the genericization moment. Any competitor could run this line.

##### Instead

Name the specific collector and the specific capability: 'For the collector who tracks comps and keeps pieces they'll never sell' or 'Field-level depth across everything you collect.'

##### Pattern

'For all your collecting needs' / 'Everything a collector could want'

#### Item 3

##### Why

Empathy by assertion instead of empathy by demonstration. The phrase claims understanding without proving it. The collector who just lost 45 minutes to a sync error doesn't need you to label their emotion — they need you to name what happened and fix it. 'We understand your frustration' is the sentence that brands use when they don't understand enough to be specific.

##### Instead

Describe the specific impact: 'Your sync failed and 12 items didn't update. Here's what happened and what we're doing about it.' Accuracy is more empathetic than any adjective.

##### Pattern

'We understand how frustrating this must be'

#### Item 4

##### Why

Two violations compressed into one phrase. 'Passionate' is banned — it's performing emotion instead of demonstrating it. 'Join our community' frames the collector as a recruit being enlisted rather than a peer being recognized. And 'our community' claims ownership of something that belongs to the collectors inside it.

##### Instead

'A community where your collection is your identity and no one needs you to explain what NFST means.' Describe what the community contains. Let the collector decide whether to enter.

##### Pattern

'Join our community of passionate collectors'

#### Item 5

##### Why

Generic tech-product language that could describe any app in any category. The organized obsessive has read these exact phrases on every collector app that disappointed them. They carry zero information and signal that the writer had nothing specific to say. They are the linguistic equivalent of stock photography.

##### Instead

Name the specific experience: 'Catalog once, see comps across sources without re-entering data.' Name the specific interface decision: 'The fields surface automatically by subcategory.' Name the specific technology: 'Dynamic field intelligence built from collector-validated taxonomies.'

##### Pattern

'Seamless experience' / 'intuitive interface' / 'cutting-edge technology'

### Watch for

Six drift vectors require ongoing monitoring. (1) Warmth overcorrection: the gravitational pull toward declared emotion ('we can't believe it,' 'this means so much') instead of structural warmth visible in what the voice notices and protects. Apply the swap test — could you replace 'Vitrine' with a competitor name and nothing breaks? If yes, the warmth is generic. (2) Wit calcifying into shtick: when every communication reaches for a punchline, the cleverness becomes wallpaper. Notifications, confirmations, and transactional emails should be clean and direct. Reserve the sharpest observations for contexts where they earn their weight. (3) Jargon creep into tech-speak: the product IS technically sophisticated, and the temptation to let the architecture speak in its own language ('multi-source aggregation model,' 'dynamic field rendering') is constant. Translate tech jargon into collector-native language in every collector-facing context. (4) Template gravity in transactional emails: welcome sequences, confirmations, and notifications have the strongest templated precedents in every writer's muscle memory. These are the highest-volume, lowest-attention places where voice erosion happens fastest. Review every transactional email against the same voice standard as a homepage rewrite. (5) Superlative creep under performance pressure: when growth metrics are reviewed, the temptation to upgrade 'field-level depth' to 'the most advanced field intelligence in collectibles' will feel like smart marketing. It's the first step toward copy that's interchangeable with every funded competitor. (6) Honesty fatigue: the specificity of 'coming Q3' and '2,300 collectors' requires ongoing discipline. The moment 'we're the honest brand' becomes a talking point rather than a practice — the moment someone says 'close enough' about a rounded number — the only thing that makes the voice defensible has been compromised.

## Frameworks notes

Three design decisions and one operational note. First: every framework in this document bottoms out in the same structural move — name the specific before naming the product. The Gap Reveal names the industry failure. The Reframe names the tool's inadequacy. The Sunday Night names the ritual. The Field Proof names the subcategory distinction. This isn't a stylistic preference; it's the operational expression of the brand's deepest positioning principle — that the organized obsessive trusts recognition over claims. A junior writer following these frameworks will produce copy that leads with the collector's world, even if they've never spoken to a collector, because the formula structure forces it. Second: the tagline system and the headline frameworks share a vocabulary layer but serve different functions. The taglines are identity compression — six words maximum, designed to trigger recognition in someone who already resonates. The headline frameworks are argument structures — designed to earn attention from someone who hasn't decided to listen yet. Campaign variants bridge the two: they borrow tagline phrases but deploy them within framework logic. Writers should understand that a tagline is not a headline. A tagline placed in a headline slot will feel like a bumper sticker. A headline placed in a tagline slot will feel cluttered. Third: the promotional messaging patterns are deliberately restrained compared to industry convention. No urgency mechanics, no social proof pressure, no emotional preambles. This will produce lower click-through rates on day one and higher trust signals over time. The entire acquisition model depends on peer recommendation from the organized obsessive, and peer recommendation only happens when the recommender trusts that every piece of Vitrine communication reflects the same honesty standard they've verified in the product. One promotional email with 'limited time' language can break that chain. Operational note: writers who don't collect should spend meaningful time in collector forums, subreddits, and Facebook Groups before writing in this voice. These frameworks provide structure, but the intuition that separates 'this term belongs here' from 'this term is being deployed here' comes from immersion. The frameworks constrain what can be written. Only collector fluency ensures what's written sounds like it belongs.

## Product messaging

### Headline formula

[Specific collector problem or status-quo friction — under 12 words]. [How Vitrine resolves it at the product level — under 12 words, naming the specific mechanism, not an adjective].

### Headline examples

- Three browser tabs of comps every Sunday night. One dashboard that consolidates them all.
- Generic fields that treat a jersey like a sealed box. Dynamic fields that know the difference.

### Feature callout formula

[Name the specific behavior or workaround the feature replaces — one sentence]. [Describe what the feature does in collector-native language, anchored to a concrete example — one to two sentences]. No adjective-first construction. The workaround IS the setup; the feature IS the payoff.

### Feature callout examples

- You built 30 columns in a spreadsheet because no app offered the right fields. Vitrine's dynamic fields surface automatically by subcategory — a game-worn jersey asks for provenance and wear documentation, a sealed box asks for seal condition and print run. The architecture already knows what to ask.
- You check comps across three browser tabs every Sunday night and mentally reconcile the numbers. Value tracking consolidates comp sources and maps recent sales to the specific variant, condition, and authentication status you've already cataloged. The Sunday-night ritual takes three minutes instead of thirty.
- Your collection photos live in a camera roll between grocery lists and parking spots. Showcases present your pieces with gallery-quality treatment — arranged the way you'd walk someone through the collection in person, displayed the way they look in your head.

### Short description example

Your collection lives across a spreadsheet, three apps, and a camera roll that's half archive, half chaos. Vitrine is one place for everything you collect — with documentation fields that change by subcategory, gallery-quality presentation, and comps consolidated from the sources you're already checking manually.

### Long description structure

Paragraph 1: Name the collector's current reality with enough precision to trigger recognition — three to four specific pain points drawn from their actual workflow (the spreadsheet columns, the Sunday-night comp check, the dead apps, the camera roll). Paragraph 2: Introduce Vitrine as the resolution by walking through what the experience looks like — catalog an item, watch the fields surface, see the presentation, check consolidated comps. Specific enough that the reader can picture using it without opening the app. Paragraph 3: Name what Vitrine doesn't do or hasn't finished — honest limitations, categories still in development, planned features described as planned. Close with the invitation to test: 'Catalog one item. The fields will tell you whether we got it right.'

### Short description structure

Open with the collector's fragmented status quo in one sentence — name the specific tools, behaviors, or workarounds. Follow with what Vitrine provides as the resolution, using one specific product detail that makes the capability testable. No adjectives. No superlatives. Two sentences maximum.

## Ctas and microcopy

### Microcopy

#### Error tone

Direct, specific, zero humor. Name what happened, what it affected, and what to do next. The voice's precision is most valuable when something breaks. Never 'Oops!' Never 'Something went wrong.' The collector needs clarity, not personality.

#### Error example

That photo didn't upload — file exceeds 10MB. Resize and try again, or email support@vitrine.com if the issue persists.

#### Empty state tone

Invitation without pressure. Acknowledge the blank space, describe what it becomes when populated through one specific example, and give a clear first action. No guilt, no over-enthusiasm.

#### Confirmation tone

Data-first, brief. Confirm what happened with specific evidence — the number of fields, the item saved, the update applied. No celebration, no exclamation points. The confirmation is the reward.

#### Empty state example

No items cataloged yet. Start with the piece you know best — the fields will show you how deep this goes.

#### Confirmation example

Item saved. 14 fields documented. Your showcase updates automatically.

### Cta principles

- Verb-forward, specific about what happens next. The CTA names the action the collector will take, not how they should feel about it. 'Start cataloging' not 'Get started!' 'See the fields' not 'Learn more.' No exclamation points in any CTA, ever.
- No manufactured urgency or social proof pressure. No 'limited spots remaining,' no 'join 10,000 collectors,' no 'don't miss out,' no countdown timers. Clicking should feel like a decision the reader made, not an impulse they were manipulated into.
- The CTA must be honest about what follows the click. If clicking leads to a signup form, the CTA says 'Create a free account' — not 'See your collection' when the collection doesn't exist yet. The moment after the click must deliver what the CTA promised.

## Headline frameworks

### Item 1

#### Formula

Every [industry player/platform type] says '[common claim].' [Specific evidence the claim doesn't hold — one sentence]. [Short reframe — under 8 words].

#### Category

brand, competitive positioning, landing pages

#### Examples

- Every collectibles app says 'built by collectors, for collectors.' Very few can explain why a game-worn jersey needs different fields than a factory-sealed box. One's a bio line. The other's architecture.
- Every platform claims cross-category support. Most of them mean one interface with generic fields wearing different labels. A label is not a category.
- Every collector tool promises 'comprehensive tracking.' None of them know that a PSA 9 Jordan rookie and a BGS 9.5 Jordan rookie are different comps. Comprehensive starts at the field level.

#### When to use

When positioning Vitrine against the status quo without naming competitors. Homepage sections, blog openers, ad copy where the audience needs to see the industry's failure before Vitrine's solution.

#### Framework name

The Gap Reveal

### Item 2

#### Formula

Your [familiar tool/behavior/system] isn't [what the collector calls it] — it's [what it actually is, with a specific and slightly wry observation].

#### Category

product, social, acquisition ads

#### Examples

- Your spreadsheet isn't a collection management system — it's a cry for help with conditional formatting.
- Your camera roll isn't an archive — it's 40% collection photos and 60% grocery lists, and you know which ones you're afraid to scroll past in public.
- Your Sunday-night eBay ritual isn't value tracking — it's three browser tabs, a mental tally, and a prayer that you didn't miss a comp.
- That abandoned app on your phone isn't a backup plan — it's a memorial to the last platform that promised everything and shipped a generic database.

#### When to use

When naming the gap between the collector's current tool and what they actually deserve. Social posts, ad hooks, email subject lines. Works best when the reframe is specific enough to be funny and true enough to sting.

#### Framework name

The Reframe

### Item 3

#### Formula

A [item type A] isn't a [item type B]. [One sentence naming the specific field-level distinction — what documentation each requires and why].

#### Category

product, feature pages, app store

#### Examples

- A game-worn jersey isn't a factory-sealed box. One needs provenance, team attribution, and wear documentation. The other needs seal condition, production year, and print run data. Vitrine knows the difference.
- A first-edition first-print isn't a book-club edition. The binding, the dust jacket points, the print indicators — different items, different fields, different documentation entirely.
- A 1960s Speedmaster isn't a modern Submariner. Movement type, reference number, service history, provenance documentation — the fields change because the watches are fundamentally different items.

#### When to use

Whenever demonstrating field-level depth as a concrete product capability. Feature pages, app store descriptions, field-depth reveal content, acquisition ads. The most conversion-effective structure for the target audience because it's immediately testable.

#### Framework name

The Field Proof

Feature launches, pricing announcements, milestone communications, any moment where the standard corporate version exists and the audience is bracing for it. The contrast between what they expected and what they got is the trust mechanism.

## Promotional messaging

### Sale example

Pro is $9/month or $79/year. It adds unlimited cataloging past 50 items, advanced value tracking across all your categories, and priority support from a real person. We kept the product free through beta so you'd have enough time to decide if we were worth paying for. If you've cataloged enough to know — here's what changes. If you haven't, the free tier stays.

### Sale approach

Vitrine doesn't discount and doesn't manufacture urgency. When introducing pricing (Pro tier) or changing pricing, state the change with the exact number, name specifically what the collector gets, explain the reasoning without emotional framing, and give a real timeline. No 'exciting new chapter,' no 'limited time offer,' no countdown timers. The communication follows the same honesty standard as everything else: specific, candid, and respectful of the collector's ability to evaluate the trade-off. The strongest pricing message is always: 'here's what it costs, here's what you get, here's why — you decide.'

### Launch example

Value tracking is live. Not estimated values. Not algorithmic guesses based on 'similar items.' Actual recent sales of comparable items, mapped to the specific variant, condition, and authentication status you've already cataloged. We consolidate comp sources so you're not cross-referencing three browser tabs every Sunday night. If your collection has enough comp data to track, you'll see it on your dashboard now. If it doesn't yet — that's honest. We'd rather show you nothing than show you a guess.

### Launch body structure

Beat 1: State what's live in three words or fewer — the restraint signals confidence. Beat 2: Define the feature by what it's NOT before what it is — name the inferior versions (estimated values, generic fields, algorithmic guesses) the audience has experienced. Beat 3: Describe the capability through a specific example the collector recognizes — map it to their actual workflow. Beat 4: Acknowledge any limitation honestly — what's not included yet, which categories are still in progress. Beat 5: Close with the invitation to use it, not excitement about it existing.

### Never do in promotions

- Never use exclamation points, ALL CAPS, fire emojis, or hype-register language in any promotional context — not in the headline, not in the CTA, not in the social promotion of the announcement
- Never create urgency that doesn't exist — no countdown timers, no 'limited spots' without a real capacity constraint, no 'don't miss out,' no 'join X collectors who already have'
- Never frame a feature launch, pricing change, or milestone as 'exciting' — let the specific detail earn the reader's response. The word 'exciting' is the sound of a brand telling the audience how to feel instead of giving them a reason to
- Never lead a promotional message with the brand's feelings — no 'we're thrilled to announce,' 'we're proud to share,' 'we can't wait for you to try.' Open with the thing itself. The thing is the announcement.

### Launch headline formula

[Feature/capability name — two to four words, no adjectives]. [One sentence describing what it does through the specific collector behavior it changes or replaces].

### Limited edition formula

When genuine scarcity exists — beta capacity, validator spots, founding cohort size — state the real number and the real operational reason. '[Number] spots in [program] because [honest constraint: testing capacity, support bandwidth, validation rigor].' Never manufacture scarcity. Never use 'limited time' without a real, externally imposed deadline. If the limit is artificial, remove it. The organized obsessive detects manufactured urgency instantly, and it permanently disqualifies the source.

- The structural failure: name why this fragmentation exists — every platform split the collector in half (passion or portfolio, depth or breadth) and served the half that was easier to monetize. The complete collector was left invisible.

- What we built and how it's different: walk through the three structural differentiators — field-level depth (with the jersey vs. box example), gallery-quality presentation, cross-category unity — each described through a specific product experience, not an adjective
- How we prove it: the honesty architecture — real metrics or no metrics, planned features described as planned, Category Depth Status page, specialist validation before marketing. Name specific evidence the reader can verify.
- The invitation: catalog one item. The fields will tell you whether we got it right. Five minutes. No pitch survives the product test — and we'd rather have you test than trust.

### Mission statement formula

[What we exist to build] for [who we serve, defined by behavior] — where [the specific standard we hold, described as architecture not aspiration].

## Core messaging principles

### Item 1

#### Name

Status quo earns the product mention

#### The rule

Every piece of copy must describe the collector's specific current situation — the tool, the workflow, the friction — before naming Vitrine or any feature. The product enters as the resolution to a precisely described problem, never as a pitch seeking a problem to attach to.

#### Do example

Your collection lives across a spreadsheet, three apps, and a camera roll you're afraid to scroll through in public. None of them know that a game-worn jersey needs different documentation than a factory-sealed box. Vitrine does.

#### Dont example

Vitrine offers field-level depth for every category, gallery-quality presentation, and integrated value tracking for serious collectors.

#### Why it matters

This audience has heard 'we're building the best collectibles app' from every platform that subsequently shut down. Leading with the product is leading with a claim. Leading with the status quo is leading with recognition — and recognition is the only currency that earns the three seconds it takes to consider what you've built. When violated, the copy sounds like marketing. When followed, the copy sounds like diagnosis.

### Item 2

#### Name

The detail is the adjective

#### The rule

No sentence may rely on a modifier — powerful, advanced, beautiful, comprehensive, intuitive — to make its point. Every adjective is replaced with the specific, testable detail it was standing in for. If removing the modifier collapses the sentence, the sentence hasn't been written yet.

#### Do example

Actual recent sales of comparable items, mapped to the specific variant, condition, and authentication status you've already cataloged.

#### Dont example

Powerful value tracking that gives you comprehensive insights into your collection's worth.

#### Why it matters

The organized obsessive checks comps, verifies authentication, and cross-references listings as a weekly habit. They evaluate every claim by testing it. An adjective is an untestable claim. A specific detail is an invitation to verify — and verification that confirms is worth more than any adjective. Adjective-dependent copy is interchangeable with every competitor. Detail-driven copy is recognizable as Vitrine.

### Item 3

#### Name

Protect the collector, target the tools

#### The rule

All irreverence, dry humor, and pointed observation is aimed exclusively at the inadequacy of tools, platforms, and the status quo. The collector's taste, spending, knowledge, collection choices, and jury-rigged systems are treated with unconditional respect. The distinction is one word: 'Your spreadsheet isn't a collection management system' targets the tool. 'You're still using a spreadsheet?' targets the person.

#### Do example

Your spreadsheet isn't a collection management system — it's a cry for help with conditional formatting. You built something impressive in there. Now imagine that depth in a tool designed for it.

#### Dont example

Still managing your collection in a spreadsheet? There's a better way. Upgrade to Vitrine and leave the Stone Age behind.

#### Why it matters

The organized obsessive built elaborate personal systems because no platform built the right one — and they did a remarkable job with inadequate materials. Any copy that implies they should have known better, collected differently, or managed more efficiently has positioned the brand as a critic, not an ally. This audience detects the difference instantly and permanently.

### Item 4

#### Name

Every number is real, every timeline is honest

#### The rule

No metric, capability, or future feature appears in any copy unless it is verifiable with live data within one hour of being challenged. Planned features are described as planned with timeline ranges. User counts are exact or omitted. 'Thousands' when the number is 2,300 is a violation. Present-tense description of an unshipped feature is a violation. This applies to marketing, investor decks, press materials, and social posts without exception.

#### Do example

2,300 collectors. 54,000 items cataloged across 12 categories. Marketplace and Verification Hub coming Q3 — we'll share specifics as they're confirmed.

#### Dont example

Trusted by thousands of collectors managing over 50,000 items. Catalog, showcase, track value, and buy and sell through a marketplace designed around authentication.

#### Why it matters

This audience has a perfect memory for platforms that exaggerated. One inflated number erases every trust signal the brand has built. 'Technically defensible' is the wrong standard — the standard is 'would we be proud of this sentence if the real number were printed next to it.' The honesty isn't a value. It's the only viable acquisition strategy for an audience that treats a single fabrication as permanent disqualification.

### Item 5

#### Name

Wit carries insight or it doesn't appear

#### The rule

Every dry observation, wry aside, or pointed comparison must contain a substantive insight that survives if the humor is stripped away. If the joke is removed and the paragraph loses its reason for existing, the joke was covering for missing substance. In empathy, apology, or bad-news contexts, wit drops entirely — no exceptions.

#### Do example

Every collectibles app says 'built by collectors, for collectors.' Very few can explain why a game-worn jersey needs different fields than a factory-sealed box. One's a bio line. The other's architecture.

#### Dont example

Oh look, another collectibles app just shut down. Shocking. Truly. Nobody saw that coming except literally everyone. Anyway, you know where to find us.

#### Why it matters

Loupe's humor is a proof mechanism — it demonstrates insider knowledge by noticing what only a collector would notice. But humor without substance is personality without purpose, and personality without purpose calcifies into shtick. The organized obsessive reads the observation underneath the wit and decides whether the brand actually knows anything. When the humor is decorative, they see a marketing team being clever. When it carries a real insight, they see a peer.

### Item 6

#### Name

Show the subcategory, skip the superlative

#### The rule

When claiming depth, demonstrate it through a specific subcategory distinction — game-worn jersey vs. factory-sealed box, first-edition first-print vs. book-club edition, 1960s Speedmaster vs. modern Submariner — rather than asserting it through category-level superlatives. The subcategory example IS the claim. No 'most comprehensive,' 'most powerful,' or 'deepest in the industry.'

#### Do example

Select 'game-worn jersey' and the fields surface era, team, authentication provider, and game documentation. Select 'factory-sealed box' and the fields shift to production year, seal condition, and print run. That's field-level depth.

#### Dont example

The most comprehensive cataloging experience in collectibles, with hundreds of specialized fields and the deepest category intelligence ever built for collectors.

#### Why it matters

Superlatives are the language of every funded competitor's landing page. Subcategory distinctions are the language of someone who actually collects. The organized obsessive tests depth claims within five minutes by cataloging an item in their primary category. A superlative gives them nothing to test. A specific subcategory example tells them exactly what to look for — and when the product delivers, the claim is proven without ever being asserted.

### Item 7

#### Name

Match the voice to the stakes

#### The rule

The voice's default register — dry, observational, faintly amused — is manually overridden in any context where the collector is experiencing loss, disruption, or concern about their data or collection value. In these moments: no humor, no personality performance, no observational asides. Only specific facts, clear next steps, and a real human to contact. The precision remains. Everything else steps aside.

#### Do example

Vitrine was down for 47 minutes this morning — 6:12 to 6:59 AM ET. Your collection data is intact. Full incident report here: [link]. If anything looks off, email support@vitrine.com. Real person, before end of day.

#### Dont example

Well, this is awkward. We had a little hiccup this morning — everything's back now. Apologies for any inconvenience. The status quo in uptime, everyone.

#### Why it matters

A voice that only works when things are going well is a performance, not a voice. The outage notification, the comp-value drop alert, the third-party-source shutdown email — these are when the audience is paying the closest attention. Wit in these moments reads as dismissiveness. The voice's sacrifice is honored by letting the humor drop entirely, revealing the directness and specificity underneath as the most powerful version of the brand.
