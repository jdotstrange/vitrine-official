## The Status Quo

**Section label:** What we saw

**Headline:**
> Every tool in collectibles was built from the wrong starting point.

**Body:**
> Some started with a database and bolted collecting onto it. Some started with a marketplace and treated the collection as a transaction history. Some started with a portfolio model and saw every piece as a ticker symbol. Each built competently from their starting point. None started from how a serious collector actually thinks, documents, values, and presents what they own.
>
> The result is a collector base managing their most significant pursuit across jury-rigged systems — spreadsheets with custom formulas that took longer to build than some of the pieces took to find, camera rolls that are half archive and half chaos, Facebook Groups where half the posts are scam accounts, and an average of two to three abandoned apps that promised everything and delivered a generic database.
>
> This wasn't a technology gap. It was an understanding gap. The tools weren't built by people who had hit the wall where the tools break — the 500-item threshold where the spreadsheet formula corrupts, the moment you try to show someone your collection and realize no screen reflects what you've built.

[CHANGED from: "Before Vitrine, this was the stack. / Every collector knows this pain. We lived it too."

The original problem section was five bullet points — "Spreadsheets: Built for accountants. Not collectors," "Facebook Groups: Great for drama. Terrible for deals," etc. Three issues. First: surface-level. "Great for drama. Terrible for deals" is snarky social copy energy, not Loupe's precision. Second: "Apps That Don't Get It — Built by people who've never waited in line for a drop" is a hype-culture reference — waiting in line for drops is exactly the audience behavior the brand rejects. The organized obsessive doesn't wait in line for drops. They wait for the right game-worn jersey to surface after months of watching. Third: the closing quote "There has to be something better" is the generic startup-origin beat every pitch deck uses.

The rewrite names the structural failure — every platform started from the wrong premise (database, marketplace, portfolio) — and describes the collector's reality with the insider specificity that creates the "that's me" recognition. The 500-item spreadsheet corruption, the camera roll, the abandoned apps — these are the behaviors the audience architecture documents.]

## The Founding

**Section label:** Why this exists

**Headline:**
> The reason no one had built the right tool wasn't technical. It was that no one with deep collecting experience and enough product obsession had tried.

**Body:**
> The tools that existed were built by software engineers who catalog, data analysts who price, and marketplace operators who transact. Each competent in their domain. None fluent in the actual practice of maintaining a serious, multi-category collection — where a game-worn jersey requires fundamentally different documentation than a factory-sealed box, and where both deserve to be seen with the kind of care their owner puts into them.
>
> Vitrine was created to be the first platform that started from the collector's actual workflow — the Sunday comp check, the subcategory-specific documentation, the desire to present pieces the way they exist in the collector's head — and built every layer of architecture to serve it. Not a database with a collecting skin. Not a marketplace bolting on inventory. A collector-native environment from the data model up.

[CHANGED from: "We decided to build it ourselves. / Vitrine started the way most good ideas do — out of frustration. We were collectors first. Cards, sneakers, watches. The usual suspects..."

The original founding section had the right instinct — establish collecting credentials — but executed it with startup-casual energy that undermines the voice. "Cards, sneakers, watches. The usual suspects" is performative casualness. "Why isn't there an app that just... gets it?" is the ellipsis-with-question-mark softness the punctuation conventions explicitly ban. "Built by collectors, for collectors" — the brand system flags this as the single most overused claim in the category, one that every dead app has used and that the organized obsessive tests within five minutes.

The rewrite makes the case through architectural specificity: the jersey-vs-box distinction, the Sunday comp check, the collector's-head presentation standard. These are the details that prove collecting fluency without claiming it.]

## What We Committed To

**Section label:** What we built — and how

**Headline:**
> Three commitments no competitor would make.

**Commitment 1 — Field-level depth over database breadth**
> Rather than indexing millions of items with generic fields, Vitrine builds dynamic documentation that changes by subcategory. A game-worn jersey and a factory-sealed box surface completely different fields — because they require completely different documentation, and any collector knows it.
>
> No category gets marketed until a specialist collector in that space validates the taxonomy. Slower expansion. Fewer categories on the marketing page. A depth standard that's publicly visible.

**Commitment 2 — Gallery-quality presentation as architecture**
> Every surface that displays a collector's items is held to the standard of a curated exhibition. A spreadsheet row is a form of disrespect to a piece someone spent years finding and thousands of dollars acquiring. The visual standard runs through every surface — catalog, showcases, social sharing, profile.

**Commitment 3 — Radical, verifiable honesty**
> Real metrics or no metrics. Planned features described as planned — in marketing, in investor conversations, on this page. When Vitrine has modest numbers, we share them honestly or display nothing. In a category where every startup inflates to survive, this commitment costs real credibility in fundraising rooms. We made it anyway — because the alternative is joining the graveyard of platforms that lied their way in.

[CHANGED from: "Collectors deserve better" section with $400B+ market / 90M+ collectors / "0 platforms built right. Until now."

Three violations in three stats. The $400B and 90M figures are unverified market-size claims presented as social proof. "0 platforms built right. Until now" is the exact superlative arrogance the brand personality rejects — "Confidence without specificity is arrogance." The line also implicitly claims every other platform was built wrong, which isn't the brand's position. The position is that no other platform built for the complete collector — a specific gap, not a universal failure.

The replacement — three specific commitments — comes directly from the brand narrative's commitment section. Each commitment is concrete, testable, and structurally harder to claim than "collectors deserve better." The organized obsessive evaluates commitments, not sentiments.]

## What We Believe

**Section label:** Beliefs

**Headline:**
> The convictions the product is built on.

**Belief 1**
> Every platform in collectibles split the collector in half — passion or portfolio, hobby or investment — and built for the half that's easier to monetize. The complete collector, the person who holds both, has never been served.

**Belief 2**
> Category depth is measured at the field level, not the label level. An app that says "Baseball" but doesn't know the difference between documenting a game-worn jersey and a factory-sealed box has built a label, not a category.

**Belief 3**
> A collection displayed as a spreadsheet row or a thumbnail in a generic grid is a collection being disrespected. Presentation is not a cosmetic layer on top of utility — it is utility for a collector whose pieces deserve to be seen the way they were meant to be seen.

**Belief 4**
> Claiming a capability that doesn't exist yet is not aspirational marketing — it's the fastest way to join the collector app graveyard. This audience verifies claims within minutes and remembers every platform that lied to them.

**Belief 5**
> Cross-category collectors are not a niche. They are the natural state of serious collecting, abandoned by an industry that found it easier to build vertically than to solve the actual problem.

[CHANGED from: Six generic beliefs — "Your collection is worth more than a spreadsheet row," "Collectors shouldn't need five apps to do one thing," "Community should amplify your collection, not complicate it," etc.

The original beliefs were sentiments — nice-sounding, unspecific, and interchangeable with any competitor. "If it's not beautiful, it's not finished" could be on Apple's About page. "Privacy is a feature, not an afterthought" could be on Signal's.

The replacement uses the five core beliefs from the Brand Constitution. Each is contrarian, specific, and structurally shapes product decisions. They aren't sentiments — they're operating principles that a collector can test against the product. "Category depth is measured at the field level, not the label level" is a testable claim. "Your collection is worth more than a spreadsheet row" is a feeling.]

# Audit Summary: About Page

## What This Page Needs To Do

The About page serves three audiences at different stages: the brand-aware collector deciding whether to trust Vitrine, the investor evaluating the team and thesis, and the press looking for a story angle. The brand narrative document defines story hooks for each. The existing page served none of them — it told a generic startup origin story that could have been about a task management app or a meal kit service with the nouns swapped out.

The restructured page tells a specific story: why the tools are broken (structural failure, not incompetence), why this person was the one to fix it (collecting fluency embedded in architecture), what they committed to that competitors wouldn't (field depth, gallery quality, radical honesty), and what the product actually believes at the operating level (contrarian, testable convictions). Every section passes the strip-the-logo test.

## Structural Changes

1. **Hero** — Replaced generic "we got tired of waiting" with the specific founding moment from the brand narrative: a collector trying to show their collection and realizing no digital tool reflected what they'd built.

2. **The Problem → The Status Quo** — Replaced five snarky bullet points with a structural analysis of why every platform failed: wrong starting premise (database, marketplace, portfolio). Added insider-specific behaviors that create recognition.

3. **The Fix → The Founding** — Replaced startup-casual origin with the specific capability gap: no one with deep collecting experience had built from the collector's actual workflow. Proves fluency through architectural specificity, not through claiming "built by collectors."

4. **The Belief → What We Committed To** — Replaced generic "collectors deserve better" sentiment (with fabricated market stats) with three concrete, testable commitments from the brand narrative.

5. **The Manifesto → What We Believe** — Replaced six interchangeable sentiments with the five core beliefs from the Brand Constitution. Each is contrarian and structurally shapes product decisions.

6. **The Vision → What's Live / What's Coming** — Replaced "Coming Soon" labels with framework for honest timeline ranges. Removed aspirational roadmap items that may not have real development plans.

7. **The Standard → Removed.** "We're not building another app. We're building the standard." is the exact superlative the brand personality explicitly rejects: "Confidence without specificity is arrogance. Confidence without honesty about limitations is the exact mistake this audience has watched a dozen platforms make."

8. **Closing CTA** — Primary tagline bookend, consistent with homepage and features page.

## The Hardest Cut

Removing "Built by collectors, for collectors" will feel wrong to the founders. It's the sentence that feels most true about their story. But the brand system is explicit: this claim has been made by every platform that disappointed the organized obsessive, and they test it within five minutes of downloading. The sentence doesn't earn trust — it triggers skepticism. The jersey-vs-box distinction, the Sunday comp check, the subcategory-specific fields — those prove collecting fluency through evidence. Evidence is the only currency this audience accepts.
